# Cinematic Clean Technology Documentary

**Working look:** Cold Steel / Warm Core  
**Language:** English narration and on-screen copy.  
**Audience:** Curious general viewers; no engineering background assumed.  
**Status:** Visual direction is inferred from the supplied still references. Editorial metrics remain provisional until full-video analysis.

## Creative north star
Make extraordinary engineering feel physically real, precise, and understandable. Use cinematic scale and lighting to earn awe; use clear visuals and measured narration to explain the mechanism. Beauty must support evidence, not replace it.

Every scene should show what a component looks like, explain how a process works, establish scale or consequence, or move a story question toward its answer.

## Visual grammar from the still references
- Wide, often symmetrical hero compositions; one dominant machine fills the frame.
- Small human figures or familiar objects give scale.
- Deep black negative space contrasts with silver, white, and graphite machinery.
- Warm amber light is localized inside a machine or at a meaningful energy source.
- Red is a small, high-salience accent.
- Thumbnail titles use large white high-contrast serif capitals with generous tracking, placed in open space above the subject.
- Mood is monumental, quiet, precise, mysterious, and clean; avoid noisy cyberpunk styling.

These stills do not show the editing, sound, narration, shot length, or transition pattern of a complete video.

## Composition and camera
Establish the whole system, isolate one subsystem, explain it, then return to the whole to show why it matters. Use symmetry when it clarifies architecture; break it to show operation or human interaction. Keep one clear focal point and intentional negative space.

Use slow pushes, slides, tilts, and macro moves only when they reveal a detail, connect components, or establish scale. Keep a stable frame during dense explanations. Avoid constant zooms, fake handheld shake, whip pans, and gratuitous parallax. Use realistic perspective and scale.

A useful visual ladder is facility -> machine -> subsystem -> component -> process -> system again. Match cuts by shape, direction, motion, or scale can connect levels.

## Color and lighting
Working palette:
- Near black: #07090C
- Graphite: #171B20
- Steel: #AAB4BC
- White text/highlight: #EEF1F3
- Amber core: #F2A45A
- Signal red: #E83B32
- Optional technical blue: #4B86B4, only when the subject needs it

Keep blacks deep but preserve readable detail. Keep silver and white metal close to neutral. Keep amber localized. Reserve red for one or two meaningful cues: person/scale, danger, selected component, or energy path. Keep skin natural and do not force every image to monochrome.

## Type and captions
### Thumbnail
Use one short title or scale-defining phrase. Set it in a high-contrast serif such as Cormorant Garamond Semibold or Libre Baskerville, uppercase, white, with generous tracking. Keep the machine unobstructed. Check font licensing. Avoid arrows, badges, emoji, and clutter unless the story truly needs them.

### In-video graphics
Use a functional sans such as Inter or Manrope for labels, numbers, subtitles, and diagrams. Keep information concise, aligned, and high contrast. White/gray is the default; amber or red marks a meaningful value or selected part. Do not duplicate narration word-for-word as decorative text.

If burned-in captions are used: sentence case, no more than two lines, consistent lower safe area, subtle shadow or dark backing, accurate punctuation and timing. Avoid karaoke word animation for long-form documentaries.

## Editing rhythm and transitions
Provisional starting targets, to be revised after viewing 3-5 complete references:
- Hook: a strong image and a specific question within 0-15 seconds; state the promise shortly after.
- Ordinary B-roll: begin at 4-7 seconds average.
- Complex diagrams, hero frames, or reveals: 6-12 seconds when needed for comprehension.
- Detail inserts: 2-4 seconds only when they add a fact or a deliberate beat.
Do not cut to a metronome. Let narration, motion, and comprehension determine pace.

Default to clean hard cuts. Also allow motivated match cuts, continuous moves into a clearly identified diagram, and short dissolves for a genuine time/location change. Avoid glitch effects, spins, flash frames, generic HUDs, repeated whooshes, and trailer booms.

## Graphics and factual clarity
Show one primary idea per diagram. Add labels in stages. Use consistent units, arrows, line weights, orientation, and color meanings. Verify machine internals and scientific behavior against primary sources. Mark a reconstruction or simplified model when a viewer could confuse it with recorded footage. Never present generic footage of a different machine as evidence of the featured machine.

## Sound and narration
Narration is clear, close, measured, and curious. Remove accidental long gaps but keep intentional silence around a reveal. Use restrained atmospheric music, subtle mechanical ambience, and precise effects. Duck music under the voice. Avoid constant sub-bass and a whoosh on every cut. Check intelligibility on phone speakers.

## Flexible story shape
1. Hook: striking image plus unresolved question.
2. Promise: what the viewer will understand.
3. Orientation: name the technology and scale.
4. Mechanism: explain one component/process at a time.
5. Constraint: reveal what makes the system difficult.
6. Consequence: connect it to people, industry, science, or a larger system.
7. Return/payoff: revisit the opening image with new understanding.

Do not force identical act lengths on every episode.

## Review checklist
Can a viewer identify the subject and focal point? Does each cut add evidence, explanation, scale, or story movement? Are real and illustrative images distinguishable? Are sources and rights logged? Are the blacks detailed, metal neutral, amber/red meaningful, and text mobile-readable? Can the voice be understood over music? Are facts and technical visuals verified?
